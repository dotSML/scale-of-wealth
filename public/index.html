Let's put this wealth in perspective by comparing it to some familiar things.
Forty years of work at the 2025 US median annual wage ($50,980 a year): about $2.04 million before tax. No raises, no investment returns. Just work.
One year of employer-sponsored family health insurance: $26,993 in 2025, including the employer and worker shares. That is just the premium.
A year at $23 an hour: $47,840 before tax, at 40 hours a week for 52 weeks. Amazon says average US fulfillment and transportation pay is now more than $23 an hour.
Give $24,000 to each of the 32,495 veterans counted as homeless in January 2025: $779.88 million. An illustrative housing grant, before support services and administration.
The entire US National Cancer Institute budget for fiscal year 2026: $7.35 billion.
Elon's fortune is about 124 times this entire annual cancer research budget.

Malaria can be prevented and treated. Bed nets, vaccines, testing and medicine save lives. Getting them to everyone who needs them takes money, trained people and functioning health services.

WHO reported just $3.9 billion invested in 2024, against a $9.3 billion annual target for 2025. Ten years at that target would be $93 billion: about 1.4% of these fortunes. This illustration holds the annual budget constant; it is not a price tag for guaranteed eradication.

That estimate works out to about 1,190 young children dying each day in the African Region. The funding target is tiny beside these fortunes. The loss is not tiny to their families.

What could we do with 2.5% of this money?

2.5%

Provide $1,200 to every American household.

There were 134.79 million US households in 2025. A one-time payment of $1,200 to each would total $161.748 billion, about 2.5% of $6.6 trillion. That is the transfer itself, before administration.

Compare the payment with the money available. One bar is every household receiving $1,200. The other is the wealth of 400 people.

For one household, $1,200 can be breathing room. For these fortunes, the entire nationwide payment is a small fraction.

5.4%

Give $10,000 to every American counted in poverty.

The latest Census poverty report counted 35.9 million people in poverty in 2024. If they were a state, they would outnumber Texas. The state populations below are 2025 estimates; the poverty count is for 2024.

US state populations (2025) compared with poverty (2024)

A one-time $10,000 payment to each of those 35.9 million people would cost $359 billion. About 5.4% of these fortunes. That is a direct cash grant; it does not mean every recipient would permanently escape poverty.

A cash payment can mean a repaired car, a cleared bill, a rental deposit or a little time to find better work. Those are ordinary things. Going without them can trap a family for years.

Research on cash transfers finds benefits in areas such as consumption, assets and well-being, although results vary by programme and place. A payment can help. Lasting security also needs housing, healthcare and dependable income.

The $10,000-per-person example and the $1,200-per-household example are separate possibilities. The larger $10,000-per-household payment later on replaces the smaller household payment in the final total.

This would not solve every problem. Some people face debts or medical costs far larger than the payment. But a sum that barely dents these fortunes could give millions of people room to breathe.

What could we do with 1.1% of this money?

1.1%

Fund ten extra years of US cancer research.

The National Cancer Institute’s 2026 budget is $7.35 billion. Ten times that is $73.5 billion, about 1.1% of these fortunes. Think of it as ten extra annual budgets at today’s level, without assuming future inflation or promising a cure.

What could we do with 3% of this money?

3%

Invest $200 billion in safe water and sanitation.

In 2024, 2.1 billion people lacked safely managed drinking water. Another measure of the same crisis: 3.4 billion lacked safely managed sanitation. Safe water means water available when needed, on the premises, and free from contamination.

About one in four without safely managed drinking water

The red slice shows 2.1 billion out of a world population of roughly 8.2 billion. A $200 billion investment would be about 3% of these fortunes. This is an illustrative fund for water and sanitation, not a claim that one cheque solves it worldwide. WHO’s 2026 assessment stresses that services also need capable institutions, maintenance and reliable financing.

What could we do with 20.4% of this money?

20.4%

Give $10,000 to every American household

Using the 2025 count of 134.79 million households, $10,000 for each one would cost $1.3479 trillion. About 20.4% of the wealth in the latest Forbes 400 list.

For a household, $10,000 can change the next year of their life. For these 400 people, all those payments combined would still leave almost four-fifths of their collective wealth.

There is an enormous amount of wealth in this picture. The question is what we choose to do with it.

What could we do with 26.5% of this money?

Combine five examples: $10,000 per household, the UN’s launch appeal, ten malaria budgets, ten extra cancer research budgets, and the $200 billion water fund. Total: $1.7474 trillion. This bundle excludes the separate poverty payment and the smaller $1,200 household payment.
About $4.85 trillion would remain. And all 400 would still be billionaires under an equal percentage contribution, using the 2025 list’s $3.8 billion minimum fortune.
These sums compare the scale of possible spending with estimated asset wealth. Selling assets, collecting revenue and delivering services would take time and work. But is it really so radical to ask whether fortunes this large could do more for everyone else?

We cannot accept this level of inequality any longer.
